The Solar System: The Sun, Eight Planets and Beyond

The Solar System is much more than eight planets. It is a gravitational family of the Sun, planets, moons, asteroids, comets and distant icy worlds. Explore how these regions fit together.

Content reviewed: 8 November 2026 · Educational overview with links to primary scientific sources

The Solar System is the gravitational system centered on the Sun. Planets and smaller bodies orbit it, while moons orbit planets and other objects. The Sun contains nearly all the mass of the system, so its gravity dominates the large-scale arrangement.

Planet order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. Pluto is classified as a dwarf planet, not one of the eight major planets.

An astronomical unit (AU) is approximately the average distance between Earth and the Sun, about 149.6 million kilometres. These figures describe average orbital scale; actual planet-to-Sun distance changes along each elliptical orbit.

Inner rocky planets and outer giant planets

Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are terrestrial planets with solid surfaces and relatively high densities. Beyond Mars lies the main asteroid belt. Farther out are four giant planets: Jupiter and Saturn are dominated by hydrogen and helium, while Uranus and Neptune contain larger proportions of volatile compounds such as water, ammonia and methane in their interiors. This is why they are called ice giants.

Rocky planets

Small, dense worlds with solid surfaces. Earth is the only world where life has been confirmed.

Gas giants

Jupiter and Saturn have extensive hydrogen-helium atmospheres, rings and many moons.

Ice giants

Uranus and Neptune contain larger shares of water, ammonia and methane compounds than the gas giants.

Asteroid belt, Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud

The main asteroid belt lies mostly between Mars and Jupiter and contains rocky and metallic bodies of many sizes. Beyond Neptune is the Kuiper Belt, a broad region of icy objects that includes Pluto. Much farther away, the Oort Cloud is a hypothesized roughly spherical reservoir that may supply many long-period comets. Its structure has not been directly mapped in detail.

How did the Solar System form?

The leading model says that about 4.6 billion years ago a cloud of gas and dust collapsed under gravity. The central region formed the young Sun, while leftover material settled into a rotating disk. Small particles collided and accumulated into larger bodies, eventually building planetesimals and protoplanets. Temperature differences across the disk helped determine which materials could condense at different distances.

Is the Solar System moving?

Yes. Earth orbits the Sun, and the Sun with its planets travels around the centre of the Milky Way. The galaxy itself is part of the Local Group and larger cosmic structures. Our motion through space therefore happens on several scales at once.

Frequently asked questions

How many planets are in the Solar System?

There are eight recognized planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

Why is Pluto not a major planet?

Pluto is round and orbits the Sun, but it has not cleared its orbital neighbourhood. The International Astronomical Union classifies it as a dwarf planet.

Which planet is farthest from the Sun?

Neptune is the most distant of the eight planets.

Does Earth's distance from the Sun stay constant?

No. Earth's orbit is slightly elliptical, so its distance changes during the year. One AU is a reference average.
