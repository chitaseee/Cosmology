The Eight Planets of the Solar System, in Order

From small Mercury to distant Neptune, every planet is a different world. This guide compares their order, composition and defining features.

Content reviewed: 8 November 2026 · Educational overview with links to primary scientific sources

Our Solar System has eight major planets. Their order from the Sun is based on their average orbital distance, although each planet moves along an elliptical orbit. The four inner planets are rocky; the four outer planets are giant worlds.

Remember the order: Mercury → Venus → Earth → Mars → Jupiter → Saturn → Uranus → Neptune.

1. Mercury

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. It has only a very thin exosphere rather than a dense, stable atmosphere, so its surface experiences extreme temperature changes between day and night. Its heavily cratered surface preserves a long record of impacts.

2. Venus

Venus is similar to Earth in size but has a very different environment. Its thick carbon-dioxide atmosphere traps heat through an intense greenhouse effect, making the surface hotter than Mercury's. Clouds of sulfuric-acid droplets and crushing surface pressure add to the hostile conditions.

3. Earth

Earth is the only place where life has been confirmed. Stable surface liquid water, a protective atmosphere, a magnetic field and active geology all contribute to its habitable environment. The Moon is Earth's natural satellite.

4. Mars

Mars looks red because iron-bearing minerals in its surface dust have oxidized. It is cold today and has a thin atmosphere, but ancient river valleys, deltas and lake deposits show that liquid water once shaped parts of the planet. Robotic missions investigate its climate history and past habitability. Read our complete Mars guide.

5. Jupiter

Jupiter is the largest planet and is composed mainly of hydrogen and helium. Its Great Red Spot is a long-lived atmospheric storm. The four Galilean moons—Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto—are worlds of interest for volcanism, subsurface oceans and planetary evolution.

6. Saturn

Saturn is famous for its broad rings, made of countless icy and rocky particles. The rings are not a solid sheet; they contain gaps and intricate structures. Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid hydrocarbons on its frigid surface.

7. Uranus

Uranus rotates with an unusually large axial tilt, producing extreme seasonal patterns. Methane in its atmosphere contributes to its blue-green appearance. It is an ice giant rather than a gas giant like Jupiter or Saturn.

8. Neptune

Neptune is the farthest major planet from the Sun. Methane absorbs some red light, contributing to its blue appearance, and its atmosphere has exhibited extremely fast winds and large storms.

Why is Pluto a dwarf planet?

In 2006, the International Astronomical Union adopted a definition requiring a planet in our Solar System to orbit the Sun, be nearly round under its own gravity and clear its orbital neighbourhood. Pluto meets the first two criteria but not the third, so it is classified as a dwarf planet.

Frequently asked questions

What is the largest planet?

Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System.

What is the hottest planet?

Venus has the hottest planetary surface because its dense atmosphere produces an extreme greenhouse effect.

Which planets have rings?

All four giant planets—Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune—have ring systems, although Saturn's are the most prominent.
