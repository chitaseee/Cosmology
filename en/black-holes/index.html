What Is a Black Hole? A Guide to Event Horizons and Beyond

A black hole is a region of spacetime with gravity so strong that nothing can escape from inside its event horizon. It is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner; astronomers study it through its effects on nearby matter and light.

Content reviewed: 8 November 2026 · Educational overview with links to primary scientific sources

A black hole is an extremely compact object whose gravity creates a boundary called the event horizon. Once something crosses that boundary, no signal—even light—can travel back out. The black hole itself is therefore dark in ordinary light, but its influence on nearby stars, gas and spacetime can be measured.

In one sentence: The event horizon is a boundary beyond which escape to the outside universe is impossible; it is not a solid surface like the ground of a planet.

What is the event horizon?

The event horizon is the boundary separating events that can still influence the distant universe from those that cannot. For a simple, non-rotating black hole, its characteristic size is proportional to its mass. The behaviour of light and clocks near this boundary is governed by relativity and is more subtle than everyday falling-object intuition.

How do black holes form?

One established route is the collapse of the core of a massive star after it can no longer support itself through nuclear energy generation. If the remaining core is massive enough, gravity overwhelms the known forms of pressure and collapse continues. Black holes can also grow by accreting surrounding material and by merging with other black holes.

Types of black holes

Stellar-mass

Often formed by massive-star collapse, with masses of several to many tens of Suns; heavier examples are also known.

Supermassive

With millions to billions of solar masses, these objects are found at the centres of many large galaxies.

Intermediate-mass

This proposed population bridges the gap between stellar-mass and supermassive black holes. Identifying and confirming candidates remains an active research area.

Why can the area around a black hole shine?

Gas and dust falling toward a black hole can form a rotating accretion disk. Friction and magnetic processes heat the material, allowing it to radiate intensely. In some systems, magnetic fields also help launch powerful jets. Much of the light astronomers observe therefore comes from hot material outside the event horizon, not from the black hole itself.

How do astronomers detect black holes?

  • Stellar orbits: Stars moving around an unseen compact mass reveal its gravity and help estimate its mass.
  • Hot gas: Accreting matter can emit X-rays and radiation at other wavelengths.
  • Gravitational waves: Merging black holes generate ripples in spacetime that detectors can measure.
  • Radio imaging: Very-long-baseline radio observatories can reconstruct emission structures near the event horizons of some supermassive black holes.

The black hole at the centre of the Milky Way

Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*) is a compact radio source at the centre of our galaxy. Precise measurements of nearby stellar orbits show that it contains a supermassive black hole with roughly four million solar masses. Infrared and radio observations are especially useful because interstellar dust obscures the central region in visible light. Learn more about our Milky Way galaxy.

Are black holes cosmic vacuum cleaners?

No. At a sufficiently large distance, the gravity of a black hole with the same mass is like the gravity of any other object with that mass. In a simplified thought experiment, replacing the Sun with a black hole of equal mass would not by itself make Earth's orbit collapse; Earth would lose sunlight and heat, but the distant gravitational pull would be essentially unchanged. Objects must approach closely and lose orbital energy to fall in.

Frequently asked questions

Can light escape a black hole?

After crossing the event horizon, no signal can escape according to our current understanding of physics.

Can we see a black hole?

Not as a glowing surface. We observe surrounding matter, stellar motion, gravitational waves and radio emission to study black holes.

What happens inside a black hole?

A complete description likely requires a theory of quantum gravity. General relativity predicts extreme behaviour, but the final physics of the deepest interior remains unresolved.
