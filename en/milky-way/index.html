The Milky Way: Our Home Galaxy in the Universe

The Milky Way is the galaxy that contains the Sun and Earth. The pale band in a dark night sky is the edge-on view of its disk, a vast structure of stars, gas, dust and dark matter.

Content reviewed: 8 November 2026 · Educational overview with links to primary scientific sources

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy. Its central region contains an elongated bar of stars, while spiral arms extend through the disk. The Sun is not at the centre: our Solar System lies in a local feature called the Orion Spur, between the larger Sagittarius and Perseus arms.

Scale: The Milky Way's stellar disk spans more than 100,000 light-years. Light would take over one hundred thousand years to cross that distance.

The main parts of the Milky Way

  • Disk: A flattened, rotating region containing much of the galaxy's stars, gas and dust. The spiral arms are part of this disk.
  • Central bar and bulge: A dense central region containing older stars and a bar-shaped structure.
  • Halo: An extended region surrounding the disk, containing globular clusters, scattered stars and most of the galaxy's dark-matter halo.

Where is the Solar System located?

The Sun lies in the Orion Spur, roughly halfway from the galactic centre to the outer edge of the stellar disk. Because Earth is inside the disk, looking along the plane of the Milky Way reveals a dense concentration of distant stars and interstellar dust. The familiar pale band is a perspective effect: we are looking through the crowded disk from within it.

How large is the Milky Way?

The answer depends on what boundary is being measured. The bright stellar disk, extended gas and dark-matter halo have different sizes. A simple description often gives the stellar disk as more than 100,000 light-years across, but there is no sharp edge where the galaxy suddenly ends; stellar density gradually decreases with distance.

The galactic centre and Sagittarius A*

At the centre of the Milky Way is a compact radio source called Sagittarius A*. Stellar-orbit measurements show that it contains a supermassive black hole with roughly four million times the mass of the Sun. Dust obscures the region at visible wavelengths, so astronomers rely heavily on infrared and radio observations. Read our guide to black holes.

The Milky Way and the Local Group

Our galaxy is one member of the Local Group, a gravitational association that includes Andromeda, the Triangulum Galaxy and many dwarf galaxies. On larger scales, galaxies form groups, clusters, filaments and enormous low-density voids in a structure often called the cosmic web.

Can you see the Milky Way from Earth?

Under a dark sky far from artificial lighting, the Milky Way appears as a faint, cloudy band. Its glow comes from the combined light of countless distant stars that the unaided eye cannot resolve individually. Light pollution and atmospheric conditions reduce contrast and can make the band difficult or impossible to see from cities.

How long does the Sun take to orbit the galaxy?

The Solar System takes roughly 230 to 240 million Earth years to complete one orbit around the Milky Way's centre. This interval is sometimes called a galactic year. Estimates vary with the adopted model of the galaxy and the Sun's motion.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Solar System at the centre of the Milky Way?

No. The Sun is in the galactic disk, in the Orion Spur, far from the central region.

What type of galaxy is the Milky Way?

It is classified as a barred spiral galaxy.

What is the black hole at the galactic centre called?

It is called Sagittarius A*, often shortened to Sgr A*.

Is the Milky Way the only galaxy?

No. The observable universe contains an enormous number of galaxies. The Milky Way is simply our home galaxy.
