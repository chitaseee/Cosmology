Mars: A Complete Guide to the Red Planet

Mars is a cold desert world that preserves geological evidence of a wetter past. Explore its atmosphere, surface, water history and the scientific search for signs of life.

Content reviewed: 8 November 2026 · Educational overview with links to primary scientific sources

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and one of Earth's closest planetary neighbours. Its reddish appearance comes from oxidized iron minerals in rocks and dust. Today Mars is cold and has a very thin atmosphere, but ancient river channels, deltas and lake deposits show that liquid water once played an important role on its surface.

Important: No life has been confirmed on Mars. Current research looks for evidence of ancient habitable environments and possible signs of past microbial life.

Size and distance

Mars has a radius of about 3,390 kilometres, roughly half Earth's radius. Its average distance from the Sun is about 1.52 astronomical units, or 228 million kilometres. The actual distance changes along its orbit. Sunlight takes roughly 13 minutes to reach Mars on average.

How long is a day and year on Mars?

A Martian solar day, called a sol, lasts about 24 hours and 39 minutes—slightly longer than an Earth day. A Martian year is about 687 Earth days. Because Mars takes longer to orbit the Sun and has a tilted axis, its seasons last longer than Earth's.

Why is Mars red?

Iron-bearing minerals in Martian rocks and surface material have oxidized, producing rust-like compounds. Fine dust can be lifted into the atmosphere and spread over large areas, making the planet look red or orange from a distance. Local rocks also show brown, gold and tan colours.

Temperature and atmosphere

Mars has a very thin atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide, with nitrogen and argon. Low atmospheric pressure means liquid water is not stable on the surface under ordinary conditions. Temperatures vary greatly by season, latitude, altitude and time of day. A single number cannot accurately describe the climate of the entire planet.

Did Mars once have rivers and lakes?

Orbiters and rovers have found valley networks, deltas, ancient lake beds and minerals that form in the presence of water. These observations support the conclusion that parts of Mars were warmer and wetter billions of years ago. The duration and global extent of those conditions remain active research questions. Today, water is found mainly as ice at the poles and beneath the surface.

Mars's moons: Phobos and Deimos

Mars has two small, irregular moons: Phobos and Deimos. Phobos is larger and closer to Mars, and its orbit is slowly decaying. Deimos is smaller and farther away. Their shapes differ strongly from Earth's nearly spherical Moon.

Olympus Mons and Valles Marineris

Olympus Mons is the largest known volcano in the Solar System, rising roughly 22 to 25 kilometres from its base to summit. Valles Marineris is a vast canyon system extending about 4,000 kilometres. These features show that Mars is geologically diverse, not simply a featureless desert.

How do spacecraft explore Mars?

Scientists use telescopes, orbiters, landers and rovers. Orbiters map the surface and atmosphere from above; rovers examine rocks and soil at close range. Missions such as Curiosity and Perseverance investigate the planet's environmental history and past habitability. Carefully selected rock samples may help answer questions that instruments on Mars cannot resolve alone.

Frequently asked questions

Can a human stand on Mars without a spacesuit?

No. The thin atmosphere, lack of breathable oxygen, low pressure, cold and radiation make an unprotected visit impossible.

Is there water on Mars today?

Yes, mainly as water ice. Geological evidence also shows that liquid water existed on the surface in the distant past.

Has life been found on Mars?

No confirmed discovery of Martian life has been made.
