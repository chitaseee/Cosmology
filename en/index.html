Explore the universe

The Solar System

The Sun is the star at the center of our planetary system. Eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets travel through the system under the influence of gravity.

Stars and stellar evolution

Stars are hot, luminous spheres of plasma. Most generate energy through nuclear fusion in their cores. A star’s mass strongly influences how it changes over time and how its life ends.

Galaxies

Galaxies are enormous gravitational systems containing stars, gas, dust and dark matter. Our Solar System is located inside the Milky Way, a barred spiral galaxy.

Nebulae

Nebulae are clouds of gas and dust between stars. Some are regions where new stars form; others are material shed by stars near the ends of their lives.

What is the Solar System?

The Solar System is a gravitational system dominated by the Sun. The four inner planets—Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars—are rocky worlds. Beyond the asteroid belt are the giant planets: Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, while Uranus and Neptune are ice giants.

Each world offers a different environment. Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System, Saturn is famous for its rings, and Mars is studied for evidence of ancient water and its potential to help us understand planetary habitability. Earth remains the only world where life is confirmed.

Star, planet and moon: what is the difference?

A star produces energy in its interior and emits its own light. A planet orbits a star and meets specific dynamical criteria. A natural satellite, or moon, orbits a planet or another body. Earth's Moon is a natural satellite.

The Milky Way and the large-scale universe

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy. Our Solar System lies in one of its spiral-arm regions, far from the galactic center. Galaxies can gather into groups and clusters, forming an intricate cosmic web. Because light takes time to travel, observing very distant objects also means looking into the history of the universe.

What is a black hole?

A black hole is a region of spacetime whose gravity is so strong that, beyond its event horizon, nothing—not even light—can escape. Astronomers investigate black holes by observing their effects on nearby matter and light. They do not act like cosmic vacuum cleaners that pull in everything from far away.

Why explore space?

Satellites, robotic spacecraft and space telescopes help scientists study planets, moons, the Sun and distant galaxies. Space missions expand our understanding of planetary history and the universe, while technologies developed for space research can also benefit life on Earth.

Astronomy & Space Knowledge Library

Earth and the Structure of Our Planet

Earth is a rocky planet with a crust, mantle and core. Moving tectonic plates shape continents, mountains and many earthquakes.

The Moon: Phases, Eclipses and Origin

The Moon is Earth’s only natural satellite. Its phases result from our changing view of its sunlit half, not from a change in the Moon’s shape.

The Sun: Nuclear Fusion and Solar Activity

The Sun is a yellow-dwarf star. Fusion of hydrogen into helium in its core produces energy that eventually escapes as light and heat.

Mercury: The Solar System’s Smallest Planet

Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun and the smallest in the Solar System. It has no substantial atmosphere and a heavily cratered surface.

Venus: A Carbon-Dioxide Atmosphere and Greenhouse Effect

Venus is similar to Earth in size, but its thick carbon-dioxide atmosphere traps heat and creates an extremely hot, high-pressure surface.

Jupiter: The Largest Planet and Galilean Moons

Jupiter is a gas giant and the Solar System’s largest planet. The Great Red Spot is an enormous, long-lived storm in its atmosphere.

Saturn: Icy Rings and Remarkable Moons

Saturn’s rings consist of ice and dust particles of many sizes. Small moons and orbital resonances help shape gaps and waves in the rings.

Uranus: An Ice Giant Tilted on Its Side

Uranus is a blue-green ice giant whose atmospheric methane contributes to its colour. Its axis is tilted by about 98 degrees.

Neptune: A Distant Planet with Fast Winds

Neptune is the most distant recognised planet in the Solar System. One Neptunian year lasts nearly 165 Earth years.

Exoplanets: Worlds Beyond Our Solar System

An exoplanet is a world orbiting a star other than the Sun. Transit and radial-velocity measurements are two major discovery methods.

Stars: Birth, Light and Stellar Evolution

Stars form in molecular clouds. After gravitational collapse and the start of hydrogen fusion, they enter the main-sequence stage.

Nebulae: Interstellar Clouds of Gas and Dust

Nebulae are clouds of gas and dust. Some are stellar nurseries; others form from material expelled by dying stars.

The Andromeda Galaxy: The Milky Way’s Large Neighbour

Andromeda, or M31, is the nearest large spiral galaxy to the Milky Way, about 2.5 million light-years away.

The Big Bang: The Scientific Model of Cosmic Expansion

The Big Bang model says the observable universe was hotter and denser in the past and space has expanded since then; it was not an explosion into empty space.

Dark Matter: Invisible Mass and Its Gravitational Effects

Dark matter does not emit or absorb light like ordinary matter, but its gravitational effects appear in galaxies and galaxy clusters.

Dark Energy: The Accelerating Expansion of the Universe

Dark energy is the name for the unknown component associated with the accelerating expansion of the universe. Its physical nature remains uncertain.

Asteroids and Comets: Remnants of the Solar System

Most asteroids lie in the belt between Mars and Jupiter. Comets release gas and dust near the Sun, forming a coma and tails.

Solar and Lunar Eclipses: The Geometry of Shadows

In a solar eclipse the Moon blocks the Sun from an observer on Earth; in a lunar eclipse the Moon enters Earth’s shadow. The Moon’s orbital tilt prevents eclipses every month.

Auroras: Solar Particles Interacting with Earth’s Atmosphere

Auroras occur when charged particles guided by Earth’s magnetic field collide with atmospheric gases, producing colourful light.

Space Telescopes: Hubble and James Webb

Hubble observes ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared light. James Webb is optimised for infrared studies of early galaxies and exoplanet atmospheres.

Mars Exploration: Rovers and an Ancient Watery Past

Mars preserves evidence of ancient rivers and lakes. Rovers analyse rocks and soil for their chemical and mineral composition.

The Kuiper Belt: Pluto and Distant Icy Worlds

The Kuiper Belt is a region beyond Neptune containing many icy bodies. Pluto is one of its best-known members.

The International Space Station: A Laboratory in Orbit

The International Space Station is a laboratory in low Earth orbit. Research examines how microgravity affects bones, muscles, plants and materials.

New Astronomy and Space Guides

22 additional guides covering moons, the Sun, stars, cosmology and Solar System objects.

Phobos and Deimos: Mars’s Moons

Mars has two small, irregular moons. Phobos orbits closer to the planet than Deimos, and their surfaces and orbits preserve clues about the history of the Martian system.

Europa: Jupiter’s Icy Moon and Hidden Ocean

Europa’s icy shell may conceal a salty ocean. It is one of the most important targets for studying the habitability of ocean worlds.

Ganymede: The Solar System’s Largest Moon

Ganymede is Jupiter’s moon and the largest moon in the Solar System. This icy world is larger than Mercury and has an intrinsic magnetic field.

Io: The Most Volcanically Active World

Io is a moon of Jupiter and one of the most volcanically active worlds known. Jupiter’s gravity and neighboring moons heat its interior.

Titan: Saturn’s Moon with Methane Lakes

Titan is Saturn’s largest moon and the only moon in the Solar System with a dense atmosphere. Rivers and lakes of methane and ethane shape its frigid surface.

Enceladus: Water Plumes from a Hidden Ocean

Enceladus is a small moon of Saturn that ejects water vapor and ice particles from fractures near its south pole, providing evidence for a subsurface ocean.

Pluto: An Icy World in the Kuiper Belt

Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt. New Horizons revealed icy mountains, nitrogen-rich plains and a tenuous atmosphere.

Ceres: The Dwarf Planet in the Asteroid Belt

Ceres is the largest object in the asteroid belt and a dwarf planet. Salts, craters and evidence of briny water reveal its complex history.

The Asteroid Belt: Remnants of Solar System Formation

The asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter contains rocky bodies and smaller objects. Jupiter’s gravity prevented much of this material from assembling into a planet.

Meteors and Meteorites: Differences and Origins

A meteor is the streak of light produced as space material enters an atmosphere. If part reaches the ground, it is called a meteorite; these objects preserve clues to Solar System history.

The Solar Wind: A Stream of Particles from the Sun

The solar wind is a continuous flow of charged particles escaping from the Sun’s corona and interacting with planetary magnetic fields.

Solar Flares and Coronal Mass Ejections

A solar flare is a sudden release of magnetic energy. A coronal mass ejection launches a large cloud of plasma and magnetic field into space.

Red Dwarfs: The Galaxy’s Most Common Stars

Red dwarfs are low-mass stars cooler and fainter than the Sun. Their abundance means many exoplanets orbit stars of this type.

White Dwarfs: The Dense Remnants of Stars

A white dwarf is the compact remnant of a star that has exhausted its nuclear fuel. It can pack a mass comparable to the Sun into a volume about the size of Earth.

Neutron Stars: Ultra-Dense Supernova Remnants

A neutron star is the collapsed core left by some massive stars after a supernova. It can contain more than the Sun’s mass in a sphere only a few tens of kilometers across.

Supernovae: Stellar Explosions and Cosmic Recycling

A supernova is an extremely energetic explosion at the end of some stellar evolutionary paths. These events disperse elements forged in stars.

Pulsars: Precise Cosmic Clocks

A pulsar is a rotating neutron star whose beams sweep past an observer like a lighthouse, producing regular pulses.

Quasars: Brilliant Active Galactic Nuclei

A quasar is an extremely luminous region at the center of some galaxies, powered by matter accreting onto a supermassive black hole.

Gravitational Waves: Ripples in Spacetime

Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime produced by accelerating, asymmetric massive systems such as merging black holes and neutron stars.

Cosmic Microwave Background: Echo of the Early Universe

The cosmic microwave background is light released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, now observed as microwave radiation across the sky.

Galaxy Types: Spiral, Elliptical and Irregular

Galaxies are commonly grouped as spiral, elliptical and irregular. Their shapes offer clues about mergers, gas content and star-formation histories.

The Habitable Zone: Where Liquid Water May Exist

The habitable zone is the region around a star where liquid water could exist on a planet’s surface under suitable atmospheric conditions; it does not guarantee life.

How Astronomers Detect Exoplanets

Astronomers use methods such as transits, radial velocity, direct imaging and microlensing to discover planets around distant stars.

James Webb Space Telescope: An Infrared View

The James Webb Space Telescope is an infrared space observatory. It studies exoplanet atmospheres, forming stars and distant galaxies.

The Hubble Space Telescope and Modern Astronomy

From Earth orbit, Hubble observes the universe in ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared light, expanding our understanding of galaxies and stellar evolution.

The Milky Way’s Center and Sagittarius A*

The Milky Way’s center contains a dense region of stars and gas. The supermassive black hole Sagittarius A* is identified through nearby stellar orbits.

What Is a Light-Year? Measuring Cosmic Distance

A light-year is a unit of distance, not time: the distance light travels through vacuum in one year. It is used for distances to stars and galaxies.

Redshift: A Tool for Measuring Cosmic Expansion

Redshift occurs when the observed wavelength of light becomes longer. Astronomers use it to study object motion and cosmic expansion.

Dark Nebulae: Interstellar Clouds of Dust

A dark nebula is a dense cloud of interstellar gas and dust that absorbs or scatters background starlight, appearing as a dark patch.

Space Weather: How Solar Activity Affects Earth

Space weather describes changes in near-Earth space driven by solar wind, flares and coronal mass ejections. These events can affect satellites and technology.

Exoplanet Atmospheres and Spectroscopy

By analyzing starlight during a planetary transit or light passing through an atmosphere, astronomers can investigate molecules and properties of exoplanet atmospheres.

Venus’s Atmosphere: An Extreme Greenhouse World

Venus has a very dense atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide and immense surface pressure. An extreme greenhouse effect makes its surface extraordinarily hot.

Mars’s Atmosphere: Why the Red Planet Is Cold and Dry

Mars has a very thin atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide. Low pressure and the absence of a strong global magnetic field contribute to its cold, dry surface.

Jupiter’s Great Red Spot: A Giant Storm

Jupiter’s Great Red Spot is an enormous, long-lived storm in the planet’s atmosphere. Its size and shape change over time as it interacts with surrounding cloud bands.

Saturn’s Rings: Icy Particles in Orbit

Saturn’s rings consist of countless icy and rocky particles orbiting close to the planet. Saturn’s gravity and its moons shape their structure.

Uranus’s Seasons: A Planet Tilted on Its Side

Uranus’s rotation axis is tilted by about 98 degrees relative to its orbital plane. This extreme tilt produces unusually long seasons and sunlight patterns.

Neptune’s Winds: A Dynamic Ice Giant Atmosphere

Despite receiving little sunlight, Neptune has extremely fast winds and large storms. Its atmosphere helps scientists understand ice giants.

Earth’s Magnetosphere: The Planet’s Invisible Shield

Earth’s magnetic field creates a region called the magnetosphere, deflecting much of the solar wind and helping produce auroras.

Lunar Eclipses: The Moon Passing Through Earth’s Shadow

A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth lies between the Sun and Moon and the Moon passes through Earth’s shadow. It happens at full Moon near an orbital node.

Solar Eclipses: Alignment of the Sun, Moon and Earth

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between the Sun and an observer on Earth. Total, annular or partial appearance depends on lunar distance and observer location.
